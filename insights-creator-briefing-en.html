What Belongs in a Creator Briefing? Checklist for Brands

Goal, core message, guardrails, formats and deadlines: how to brief creators without taking away their own style.

In short

A good briefing sets the goal, core message, guardrails, formats and deadlines, but leaves the creator room for their own style. The clearer the guardrails, the fewer rounds of corrections.

By PANA Agency · Updated: October 16, 2026

Why the briefing decides the quality

Creators know their community best. A briefing therefore has to do two things: it tells the creator what the brand wants to achieve, and it leaves room to tell the message in the creator's own words. Overly tight rules quickly feel like advertising, while loose ones lead to content that does not fit the goal.

What belongs in a briefing

  • Campaign goal: Reach, traffic, leads or awareness.
  • Target audience: Who should the content reach?
  • Core message: What should stick, in one to three sentences?
  • Product and facts: What must be correct, and what must not be claimed?
  • Dos and don'ts: Mandatory information, taboo topics, competitors, tone.
  • Format and platform: Story, short video, YouTube integration, number of pieces.
  • Dates and deadlines: Submission for approval, publication, response times.
  • Labeling: How will the collaboration be made recognizable as advertising?
  • Usage rights: May the content be used on brand channels or in ads?
  • Contact person: Who answers questions and gives approval?

Script or framework?

For most campaigns a framework briefing works better than a fixed script: guardrails, core statements and dos and don'ts instead of pre-written sentences. This applies especially to reactive campaigns, where content is produced quickly. For regulated products, some statements are fixed, see creator marketing in regulated industries.

How a briefing works

  1. Write the briefing: Put goal, message and guardrails in writing.
  2. Align in a conversation: Go through the idea, the effort and the conditions with the creator.
  3. Clarify questions: Answer open points before the shoot.
  4. Review the draft: Approve or correct content before publication.
  5. Publish and evaluate: Record the results and use them for the next wave.

Typical mistakes

  • Too many messages: A piece of content can carry one main message well.
  • Briefing too late: Short-notice briefings lead to stress and corrections.
  • Unclear approval: Who decides by when? That belongs in the briefing.
  • No room to move: Dictating word for word rarely produces authentic content.
